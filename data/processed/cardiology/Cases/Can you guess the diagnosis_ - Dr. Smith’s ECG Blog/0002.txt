The right ventricle dilates in response to acute afterload increase. Right ventricular dilation leads to left ventricular underfilling and decreased cardiac output. The right ventricle normally receives blood supply throughout the cardiac cycle. ECG Findings in Pulmonary Embolism The ECG is not sensitive for pulmonary embolism. However, findings such as sinus tachycardia, S1Q3T3, anterior T-wave inversions, new right bundle branch block, or incomplete right bundle branch block increase likelihood. S1Q3T3 is uncommon but increases the likelihood of PE. When troponin is positive, the differential includes myocardial infarction and pulmonary embolism. In patients with precordial T-wave inversion, concomitant T-wave inversion in leads III and V1 strongly favors pulmonary embolism over acute coronary syndrome. Negative T waves in leads III and V1 are rare in ACS and common in acute pulmonary embolism. Anterior T-wave inversion is a common ECG finding in massive pulmonary embolism. Normal ECGs are uncommon in massive pulmonary embolism, but smaller emboli may present with minimal ECG changes. Interpretation Without History Without clinical history, ECG findings may appear nonspecific. When history reveals progressive dyspnea over two weeks, subtle ECG findings become clinically meaningful. Subtle ECG findings consistent with pulmonary disease include: Anterior T-wave inversion T-wave inversion in lead III S1Q3T3 pattern qR pattern in lead V1 Persistent precordial S waves Low QRS voltage Subtle ST-segment straightening or elevation in aVR These findings, taken together with clinical history, are consistent with acute submassive pulmonary embolism.
