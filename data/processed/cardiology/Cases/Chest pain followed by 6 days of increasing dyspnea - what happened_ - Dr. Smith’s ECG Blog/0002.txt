Post-Infarction Regional Pericarditis (PIRP) Post-infarction regional pericarditis is a complication of transmural myocardial infarction and is more common with delayed or absent reperfusion. ECG features suggestive of PIRP include: Persistently upright T waves more than 48–72 hours after myocardial infarction Premature reversal of inverted T waves to upright deflections in the presence of well-formed Q waves Upright T waves beyond 72 hours after acute myocardial infarction should prompt consideration of PIRP. Ventricular septal rupture results in left-to-right shunting, reduced cardiac output, pulmonary edema, and shock. Prognosis and Pathophysiology Ventricular septal rupture following myocardial infarction carries an extremely poor prognosis without surgical correction. Mechanical complications such as ventricular septal rupture, free wall rupture, and papillary muscle rupture are more likely in elderly patients, those with delayed or absent reperfusion, and first myocardial infarction without prior ischemic preconditioning. Ventricular septal rupture typically occurs 1–14 days after myocardial infarction, with bimodal peaks within 24 hours and again at 3–5 days. Necrotic myocardium is fragile, with maximal tissue breakdown around day 7. Surgical timing must balance hemodynamic stabilization with tissue integrity. Learning Points QS waves with persistent ST elevation and/or hyperacute T waves may represent either acute myocardial infarction or subacute completed infarction with post-infarction pericarditis. Post-infarction regional pericarditis implies complete transmural infarction and increased risk of myocardial rupture. Mechanical complications of transmural infarction are rare but carry high morbidity and mortality. A new harsh systolic murmur in a patient with subacute myocardial infarction suggests ventricular septal rupture or papillary muscle rupture until proven otherwise. Clinical correlation is essential in all cases.
