Primordial germs cells undergo a second wave of DNA methylation to establish parent-specific genomic imprinting, i.e., the repression of transcription selectively from the paternal or maternal allele of certain genes (Supplementary Note 9). De novo promoter CGI methylation also occurs, albeit at a very slow rate, during aging, particularly in genes that are PcG targets in ES cells.47 Permanent cell lines and cancer cells often gain CGI methylation as well.10 In fact, aberrant DNA methylation and other epigenetic changes may precede other transforming events such as mutations and genomic instability (Supplementary Note 10).33,48,49 Due to their early appearance, rarity in normal cells, and the robustness of their detectability in patient samples, hypermethylated CGIs have the promise to become useful as cancer biomarkers.50 Paradoxically, CGI hypermethylation in cancer is accompanied by hypomethylation e.g. of juxtacentromeric and other repetitive sequences, which may lead to genomic instability through illegitimate recombination and cause transcriptional deregulation of neighboring genes.48 Hypomethylation of genes with oncogenic potential has also been described in several cancers.48,49 Transcriptional repression by DNA methylation is mediated by both exclusion and recruitment of various mediators.10 Proteins that are specifically recruited to 5meCpG are the methyl-CpG-binding proteins MeCP2, MBD1-4 and Kaiso,10 which exert their functions by anchoring co-repressor complexes such as SIN3A/histone deacetylase complex or the nucleosome remodeling/histone deacetylation complex NuRD to methylated DNA.11 While these proteins perform redundant functions, de novo mutations in the X-linked MECP2 gene underlie most cases of Rett syndrome, an extreme neurological syndrome affecting female heterozygotes.
