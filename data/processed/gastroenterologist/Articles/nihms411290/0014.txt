A popular formalization of this idea is that histone modifications occurring in a combinatorial manner may define and predict key epigenetic states and thus could constitute a “histone code” that extends the information potential of the genetic code.28,29 Furthermore, nonhistone proteins playing key roles in regulating chromatin are themselves targets of numerous, functionally important post-translational modifications that could be considered a “subcode” within the context of the primary histone code.55,56 However, the histone code hypothesis does not directly take into account the fact that, in addition to covalent modifications, histones also carry information in their primary sequence,21 position14,32 and even kinetics of turnover,46 and this complexity is further Neurogastroenterol Motil. Furthermore, post-translational histone modifications may not have the longevity required for long-term memory of transcriptional patterns.46 To address this specific weakness, Allis and colleagues have proposed that their maintenance beyond a single cell cycle may instead depend on the genomic region-specific inheritance of histone variants, which preferentially bear certain marks (“histone barcode hypothesis”).30 However, the histone code hypothesis has also been criticized for generally falling short of key criteria set forth for coding systems.58,59 While debate about the existence or utility of the histone code is still ongoing, recent studies involving integrative analysis of genome-wide distribution of histone modifications, chromatin-modifying and -remodeling proteins, DNase1 sensitivity, ncRNAs and transcriptional activity were able to identify a finite number of chromatin states that correlate with the behavior of the underlying genes, indicating that predictive, functional annotation of the chromatin may perhaps be possible.60–62 Key epigenetic features of genomic regions and their spatiotemporal organization are discussed further in Supplementary Note 11.
