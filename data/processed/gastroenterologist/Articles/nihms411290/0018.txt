Epigenetic control of genes encoding neurotransmitters and their receptors defines the identities and functions of neurons85 and may hold the key to understanding selective changes in neuron populations in a variety of conditions.3 Early life experiences including exposure to infections and highly stressful events play important roles in the pathogenesis of functional gastrointestinal disorders8,86 and are likely to involve heritable, chromatin-level changes in inflammatory/immune cells, the central nervous system and the gut neuromuscular apparatus. For example, nitric oxide released during acute inflammation has been found to exert an anti-inflammatory effect by increasing the expression of histone deacetylase 3, suppressing H4K12ac and thus excluding nuclear factor κB from the promoter of the pro-inflammatory genes.87 Resident intestinal macrophages are a key component of inflammatory processes but can also contribute to antioxidant defense.88 Macrophage polarization toward pro-inflammatory vs. tolerant phenotypes strongly depends on the cytokine gene expression profile of these cells, which may reflect changes in histone acetylation.89 In turn, inflammatory insults in rats have been shown to affect smooth muscle function later in life and several weeks after the subsidence of colonic inflammation indicating a likely epigenetic component.90,91 These changes may contribute to the pathogenesis of post-infectious irritable bowel syndrome and dyspepsia. Functional pain syndromes are also part of the functional gastrointestinal disorder spectrum8 and early postnatal exposure to esophageal acid has been shown to cause prolonged upregulation, particularly upon re-challenge in adulthood, of gene expression for N-methyl- D-aspartate receptor subunits and postsynaptic density protein 95 in the cingulate cortex, which is responsible for processing pain of esophageal origin.92 The underlying changes in chromatin remain to be studied.
