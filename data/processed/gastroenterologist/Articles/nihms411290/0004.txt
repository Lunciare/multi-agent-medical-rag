Epigenetic research is mainly concerned with transcriptional regulation by cis-acting molecular signals, i.e., signals physically associated with the DNA and inherited with the chromatin during cell division. However, self-sustaining phenotypic traits can be transmitted, both horizontally and vertically, by mechanisms regulating transcription in trans (e.g., factors transmitted by partitioning of the cytoplasm)14 or by factors completely unrelated to transcriptional control (see Supplementary Note 1). However, in keeping with the approach followed by most authors, below we focus on epigenetic mechanisms concerned with establishing transcriptional patterns and propagating them through cell divisions. Component molecular mechanisms underlying epigenetic control The chromatin regulates access to the DNA template Epigenetic control is concerned with indexing the genome according to the probability of use of the genetic material for mRNA and, ultimately, protein synthesis.15 The need for such a system arose with the evolution of eukaryotes, which must package an enlarged amount of genetic material into the confined space of the nucleus (Figure 1). Condensing and indexing the genome are functions of the chromatin, a nucleoprotein complex formed by the DNA with histone and nonhistone proteins. Thus, DNA-templated processes such as gene expression, DNA replication and repair are controlled, to a great extent, by the chromatin, which must assume an “open” configuration to provide access for the RNA polymerase II complex, the replication machinery and DNA repair systems to execute these functions.16,17 Conversely, chromatin compaction contributes to the establishment and maintenance of cellular phenotypes by repressing irrelevant gene expression, compensates for gene dosage inequalities by inactivating one of the X chromosomes in females of eutherian mammals, protects the integrity of the genome by silencing viral genomes and transposable elements, reduces transcriptional “noise” by repressing noncoding and repetitive sequences, protects chromose ends (telomeres) and aids chromatid separation by providing attachment anchors in centromeres for kinetochore–microtubule assemblies during nuclear division.11,14,16,18,19 In interphase nuclei, decondensed, open or euchromatin (Figure 2) is ~10 times less dense than metaphase chromosomes and is more sensitive to deoxyribonuclease 1 (DNase1) treatment.
