Although the epigenome never becomes static during an individual’s life,64 periods of especially high chromatin plasticity exist during development that are characterized by greater sensitivity to environmental inputs.69 The first window is characterized by the erasure and reestablishment of genomic imprints in primordial germ cells following their migration into the gonadal ridge (around embryonic day 7.25 in the mouse).70 The time lapse between demethylation and remethylation is sex-dependent: in males, remethylation occurs by embryonic day 14 but in females it is delayed until maturation in the adult during each estrous cycle.69 The second window begins with a wave of global demethylation shortly after fertilization11,70,71 and ends with the remethylation of “junk” DNA and promoters of pluripotency and tissue-specific genes before the end of gastrulation.10,11,41,44 The third period of greater vulnerability is perinatal development13 when exposure to the environment increases dramatically. While epigenetic adaptability reduces with aging, certain systems remain especially sensitive to environmental changes even in the adult. For example, sustained neuronal activity and neurogenesis have been proposed to create vulnerability to epigenetic dysregulation by environmental toxicants and drugs in the adult brain,13 and the same may also hold true for the gut, which constantly experiences environmental insults including signals arising from the microbiome.4 Besides manifesting in the exposed individual, response patterns to environmental factors may be transmitted through the germ line if the gametes’ epigenome is affected. Several epigenetic signals can be inherited transgenerationally14 including maternal transcription factors, ncRNAs, histone modifications and altered DNA methylation. To be considered transgenerationally inherited, a trait must be demonstrable in at least the F generation when embryonic exposure is involved, since in this case not only the G gestating female, but also the embryo (F ) and its developing germ cells (F ) are directly exposed to the environmental 1 2 change. By the same token, after postnatal exposure, at least the F generation must be assessed. Effects of several environmental factors including heavy metals, nutrients, chemical exposure and endocrine disruptors on disease susceptibility have been studied in the F generations and beyond.45 However, multigenerational effects can also be passed 1–3 on behaviorally rather than biochemically.
