Statement 7—In ALF patients, obesity is associated with an increased risk of death or need for transplantation and an increased mortality after transplantation. (BM) Strong consensus (96 % agreement) Commentary: In ALF, there is only very limited data available regarding the effect of nutritional status on its course and prognosis. Rutherford and colleagues (107) analyzed 782 adult ALF patients prospectively enrolled from 1998–2004. They found the same prevalence of obesity (30 %) in ALF patients as in the general population and between BMI categories the proportion of patients being listed for transplantation was not different. Obese and severely obese had a 1.6- and 1.9-times higher risk of transplantation or death from ALF. Obese patients had a 3.4 times higher risk of dying after transplantation. In a small retrospective series, overweight patients were found more susceptible to ALF (108). In severely undernourished anorexia nervosa patients an ALF-like condition has been described (109) without morphologic evidence of hepatocyte necrosis (110). Patients recovered completely upon adequate re-feeding. Statement 8—In the management of severely malnourished ASH and cirrhosis patients, a poorer survival compared to non-malnourished patients shall be expected. (BM) Strong consensus (100 % agreement) Commentary: Undernourished ASH patients had a higher rate of morbidity and mortality in the reports of the combined data from the American Veteran Affairs (VA) study (111–113). The VA study data show a clear association between low intake of normal food and high mortality (111) and this finding has been confirmed recently (114). In severely malnourished LC patients a number of studies reported higher morbidity and mortality (16, 22, 115, 116) as well as a higher mortality following LT (22, 25–28, 31, 53, 115–117). Data are controversial regarding a higher prevalence of hepatic encephalopathy (HE) in malnourished LC patients (50, 118, 119).
