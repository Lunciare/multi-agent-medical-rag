Evidence for thiopurine immunosuppression49,216 and 6 biological treatments compared with conventional medical therapy55) to reduce the need for surgery 7 is equivocal. When medical treatment does not control the symptoms, surgery is then considered. A 8 wide range of procedures can be performed, that involve removal of the diseased segment of 9 intestine. Usually a surgical join (anastomosis) averts the necessity for a stoma. 10 Traditionally the operation has been carried out through an abdominal incision to enable open 11 mobilisation and removal of the intestine. In the last ten years, laparoscopic surgery has been 12 increasingly used so that by the end of 2009, just over 30% of abdominal colorectal procedures in the 13 UK were carried out in this way183,276, the rationale being that the reduced length of stay is offset by 14 the cost of the instruments and the longer duration of the procedure. 15 Surgery results in a rapid restoration of the patient’s health in most cases. Operative mortality is 16 below 1% and anastomotic dehiscence below 5%.41 A complication is more likely when perforating 17 disease with local sepsis has occurred. Recurrent inflammation in the vicinity of the anastomosis 18 occurs within one year in 70% of people with Crohn’s disease229 but this does not necessarily mean 19 surgical resection will be required. Distal ileal Crohn’s disease has a 90% likelihood of requiring 20 surgery over a 15-year period.24,81 Recurrence requiring resection has been reported to be about 30% 21 at five years, and 50% at 10 years after the first resection24,81 although lower rates of 17% at 10 years, 22 and 56% at 20 years have been reported from a population-based study.253 Long-term freedom from 23 recurrence after surgical resection of up to 50% over 10 years has been reported.54 24 Early surgery has been advocated in patients with ileocolic Crohn’s disease133, with the reasoning 25 that removal of the diseased segment is achieved before perforating disease develops.
