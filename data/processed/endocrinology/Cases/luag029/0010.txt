Similarly, our patient exhibited normal testosterone and spontaneous puberty yet azoospermia, reinforcing the dissociation between steroidogenic and sperma­ togenic compartments. Domenice et al reported a patient with a NR0B1 frameshift variant who developed peripheral preco­ cious puberty with elevated testosterone and suppressed gona­ dotropins, suggesting ACTH-driven Leydig stimulation in early life. Seminara et al reported a case with a frameshift variant (c.123_124del; p.Ser42Trpfs*12) who achieved normal puberty despite neonatal adrenal insufficiency. Testicular biopsy re­ vealed germ cell aplasia with Leydig hyperplasia—consistent with Sertoli cell failure despite preserved androgen production . These findings support the hypothesis that progressive tubu­ lar failure may occur independently of central hypogonadism in some NR0B1 variant carriers, with important implications for fer­ tility counseling. Animal models suggest Sertoli cell–intrinsic dysfunction as the primary mechanism of infertility in NR0B1-related disease. Loss of function variants in NR0B1 impair spermatogenesis independ­ ently of gonadotropin or androgen status. Mouse, medaka fish, and marsupial studies show that DAX1, also referred as Ahch in these models, is required for Sertoli cell–mediated germ cell maintenance, and its absence leads to infertility despite normal hormonal profiles .
