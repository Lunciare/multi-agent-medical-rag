The patient was diagnosed with acute pancreatitis with kidney injury after she presented with vom­ iting, an acute abdomen, elevated pancreatic amylase and lip­ ase, and elevated creatinine. Further work-up on day 3 of admission for persistence of abdominal pain despite improve­ ment in pancreatic amylase, lipase, and creatinine levels led to the detection of hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, progressive hyperpigmentation, and the diagnosis of AI. In contrast, our patient lacked hyperpigmentation and had normal serum elec­ trolyte levels at presentation and tolerated a surgery under general anesthesia without complications 6 months prior, making a diagnosis of primary AI prior to admission less like­ ly. Thorough evaluation performed following the incidental detection of adrenal enlargement in CT imaging led to the diagnosis of AI in our patient before the development of elec­ trolyte abnormalities and hyperpigmentation. AI develops when more than 90% of the adrenal cortex is destroyed. Thus, there may be a latent period between an acute adrenal insult and the onset of adrenal crisis, suggesting early identification and management can affect the outcome of patients following bilateral adrenal injury .
