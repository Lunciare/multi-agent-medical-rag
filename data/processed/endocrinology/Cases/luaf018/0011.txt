IHC staining is positive for S-100, vimentin, and neuron-specific enolase, confirming neural crest origin, but negative for cytokeratin, smooth muscle actin (SMA), and ac­ tin, distinguishing schwannomas from epithelial and smooth muscle tumors . Aberrant TTF-1 expression, seen in some cases, was observed in our patient as well . About 60% of sporadic schwannomas harbor an NF2 gene mutation, which was also the case in our patient’s schwannoma . Mutations in LZTR1 and SMARCB1 have also been de­ scribed . Molecular testing could be pursued for indeterminate FNA samples, but not all molecular tests for thyroid FNAs are capable of detecting NF1 or NF2 pathogen­ ic variants, which are commonly seen in schwannomas. Only NF2 gene mutations are detected on commercially available molecular tests (especially Thyroseq V3) that are specifically used for thyroid cytology. However, these tests can help with diagnosing MTC or ATC by identifying their character­ istic pathogenic variants (such as RET, BRAF, or TP53). Differential diagnoses include Riedel thyroiditis, MTC, ATC, spindle cell variants of thyroid carcinomas, neuro­ fibroma, leiomyoma, and malignant PNST .
