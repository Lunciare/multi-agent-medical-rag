Our patient’s QCE yielded values of 6.2% to 6.3% (44 to 45 mmol/mol) (see Table 1) across 2 years, whereas HPLC failed to report a value. CTI is the current method used to evaluate our patient’s HbA1c. This technique combines colorimetry, which uses spectrophotometry, with turbidimetric immunoinhibition, which measures deposition of antibody-tagged HbA1c turbidi­ metrically . CTI demonstrates concordance with HPLC in patients with normal Hb variants; however, in rare Hb var­ iants with altered N-terminal β-chains, HPLC yields falsely elevated HbA1c values while CTI remains accurate . However, CTI has limitations, as it relies on antibodies accur­ ately binding HbA1c molecules. This interaction is hindered by thalassemias, hemoglobinopathies, and rheumatoid factors, which alter HbA1c structure or bind the antibody itself (Table 2) . In our patient with Hb Graz, CTI resulted in HbA1c values comparable to enzymatic analysis, supporting its potential use for monitoring glycemic control.
