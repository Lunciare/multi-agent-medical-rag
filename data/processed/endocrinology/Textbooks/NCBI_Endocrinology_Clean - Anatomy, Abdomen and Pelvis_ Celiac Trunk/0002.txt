The left gastric artery, usually the smallest branch, supplies the proximal portion of the stomach along the lesser curvature and the distal esophagus, and forms an anastomosis with the right gastric artery, a branch of the common hepatic artery. The common hepatic artery gives rise to branches that supply the liver, pylorus of the stomach, distal portions of the greater and lesser curvatures, gallbladder, proximal duodenum, and pancreatic head. The splenic artery provides arterial supply to the proximal and middle segments of the greater curvature, the fundus of the stomach, a substantial portion of the pancreas, and the spleen, terminating at the splenic parenchyma. The classic trifurcation of the celiac trunk, termed “tripus Halleri,” was first described by von Haller in 1917 and is observed in approximately 89% of individuals, irrespective of sex. Two forms of trifurcation have been defined. A “true” tripod occurs when the hepatic, left gastric, and splenic arteries share a common origin, forming a hepatogastrosplenic trunk.
