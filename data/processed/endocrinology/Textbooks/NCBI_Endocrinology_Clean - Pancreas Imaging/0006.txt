Plain Films The use of plain radiographs is extremely limited in evaluating the pancreas. Although not diagnostic, calcifications of the pancreas may be visible on abdominal radiographs in the setting of chronic pancreatitis. Computed Tomography, Magnetic Resonance Imaging, and Ultrasound A few general principles common to imaging across body parts are essential for deciding which of these 3 cross-sectional modalities to use and for assessing the relative strengths and weaknesses of each. In general, computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have large fields of view and are both very effective at identifying local abnormalities and regional or more distant involvement. Ultrasound has a limited field of view and can be substantially limited by body habitus and overlying gas. Computed tomography and MRI are better than ultrasound for characterizing solid lesions, owing to the ability to administer intravenous contrast material and differences in MRI signal intensity. On the other hand, cystic lesions can be detected with all 3 modalities but are better characterized by ultrasound and even better by MRI.
