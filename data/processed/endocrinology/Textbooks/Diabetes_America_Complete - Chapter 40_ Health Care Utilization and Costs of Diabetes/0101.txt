In 2012, an estimated 25 million days of work were lost due to diabetes. The cost of missing work due to diabetes was estimated to be $5 billion. The reduced performance at work due to diabetes was estimated to represent 113 million lost work days per year, accounting for a cost burden of $20.8 billion. If people with diabetes not in the workforce had similar numbers of days during which they were unable to work because of their diabetes, this would have accounted for 20 million days of reduced productivity and would have cost $2.7 billion. People with diabetes were more likely to leave the workforce because of disability related to their diabetes, and in 2012, 130 million days of productivity were lost at a cost of $21.6 billion. Using estimates of premature mortality from the ADA publication, of all deaths in the United States in 2012, 246,000 were attributable to diabetes. This total includes 73,000 deaths with diabetes as the primary cause of death, 25,000 deaths with renal disease as the primary cause of death, 38,000 deaths with cere­ brovascular disease as the primary cause of death, and 110,000 deaths with cardio­ vascular disease as the primary cause of death (Table 40.60) (20). The average cost of premature death declines with age, because there are fewer remaining expected working years. Nevertheless, mortality attributable to diabetes accounts for $18 billion in indirect costs, or $75,100 per death.
