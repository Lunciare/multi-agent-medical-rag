The examination may reveal signs of exsanguination, such as tachycardia and hypotension, or nonspecific findings like abdominal or flank tenderness. The release of cortisol and catecholamines by the expanding hematoma may present with elevated blood pressure, especially if the underlying etiology is a pheochromocytoma. Evaluation Most patients have normal lab parameters, possibly reflecting minor adrenal bleeding. Leukocytosis and anemia may be present. Biochemical markers of adrenal insufficiency, such as hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, and hypoglycemia, may also be detected. Other important derangements include excess cortisol and elevated catecholamine levels, which may reflect the transient release from the gland destruction. Low cortisol with elevated adrenocorticotropic hormone is an important diagnostic clue signifying extensive cortical involvement. Imaging remains the most important diagnostic tool, with computed tomography scan being an important modality. The appearance of imaging varies with the age of the hematoma. Adrenal hemorrhage on computed tomography scan appears as a round-to-ovoid lesion. There may be peri-adrenal fat stranding, and bleeding may extend into the peri-nephric space.
