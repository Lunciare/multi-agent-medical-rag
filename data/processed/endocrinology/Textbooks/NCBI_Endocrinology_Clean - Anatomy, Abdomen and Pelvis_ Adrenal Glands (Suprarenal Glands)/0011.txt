Appropriate adrenal functional tests, such as the low-dose dexamethasone suppression test, plasma metanephrines, and plasma aldosterone, should be performed. If these are positive, the patient will likely need adrenalectomy. If the tests are negative, possible metastasis should be considered. If there is a history of primary cancer, a biopsy may be able to confirm metastatic disease; however, adrenal incidentalomas should not be biopsied unless there is a known history of cancer, which can lead to seeding a primary adrenal cancer from the biopsy into the surrounding tissues. Breast and lung cancer are the most common primary cancers metastasizing to the adrenal. If there is no history of cancer and the adrenal incidentaloma is less than 4 cm, CT should be repeated in 4 to 6 months. If the mass is larger than 4 to 6 cm, a functional workup should be performed, and an adrenalectomy should be considered. A biochemical work-up can identify nonfunctional masses, which are typically less than 4 cm. A functional mass-producing hormone requires medical or surgical intervention, including: Elevated aldosterone: Conn syndrome Elevated cortisol: Cushing syndrome Elevated androgens: Precocious puberty
