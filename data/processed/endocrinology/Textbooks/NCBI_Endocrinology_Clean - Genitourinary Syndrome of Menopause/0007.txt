Additionally, as the vaginal pH changes, the vaginal flora may also change, increasing the risk of recurrent urinary tract infections. Unlike vasomotor symptoms, which tend to improve over time, GSM is progressive without treatment. The anatomical changes associated with GSM can include fusion of the labia major or minora, introital stenosis, labial and clitoral atrophy, phimosis of the prepuce, and urethral caruncles, polyps, eversion, or prolapse. The urethral meatus may become susceptible to physical irritation. Vaginal changes may include tissue fragility and friability, loss of rugae, the presence of petechiae, fissures, ulcerations, decreased elasticity, reduced lubrication or secretions, and a shortened, narrowed, and poorly distensible vaginal vault. Thinning and regression of the labia minora, retraction of the introitus, and involution of the hymenal carunculae can lead to dyspareunia. Other anatomical changes associated with GSM include reduced collagen content and hyalinization, thinning of the epithelium, and decreased elastin production. Pelvic and speculum exams may cause pain and bleeding. Classic physical examination findings of atrophy may also include pale, dry, smooth, and shiny epithelium or inflammatory changes such as patchy erythema, petechiae, or increased visibility of blood
