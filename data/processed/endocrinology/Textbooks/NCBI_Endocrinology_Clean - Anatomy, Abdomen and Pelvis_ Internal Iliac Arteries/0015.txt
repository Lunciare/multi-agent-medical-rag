Careful consideration of the many anatomic variants of the internal iliac artery is important in reducing a variety of potential surgical complications. For example, in individuals with the corona mortis, compression of the internal iliac artery does not completely stop bleeding from its contributing arteries, since the external iliac artery also supplies this vascular loop. However, compression of the internal iliac artery controls the blood flow contributed by the IIA to the corona mortis . Clinical Significance The internal iliac artery conveys a relatively large volume of blood, and its branches supply numerous visceral and parietal structures in the pelvis, gluteal region, hip, and thigh. As a result, blunt force trauma, penetrating injuries, and pelvic fractures put these vessels at considerable risk with potentially life-threatening consequences. The internal iliac arteries are at risk of various pathologies that impact the arterial system. Internal iliac artery stenosis is a relatively common condition often due to atherosclerosis. This condition is typically associated with aortic or common iliac artery stenosis. Isolated occlusion of the internal iliac arteries is less common and more challenging to diagnose than aortoiliac disease.
