Despite the absence of clear renal preservation benefits in older adults with preserved baseline renal function, PTX remains a safe procedure in this population, with complication rates comparable to those in younger patients ( 46 ). Therefore, surgery remains a reasonable option for symptomatic disease or for other established indications, such as fracture risk reduction and improved quality of life. Importantly, in patients with pre-existing renal impairment, stabilization of renal function following surgery has been observed, supporting current recommendations that an eGFR below 60 mL/min constitutes an indication for PTX ( 124 ). Consequently, preservation of renal function should not be considered a primary rationale for recommending surgery in older adults with PHPT and normal renal function ( 91 ). Risk-Benefit Balance Older patients have a higher absolute risk of morbidity related to skeletal and renal complications, suggesting that they may derive substantial benefit from PTX ( 92 ). Surgical cure rates in older adults appear comparable to those in younger patients, with only a modest increase in operative risk ( 54 , 57 ). However, much of the available evidence originates from high-volume surgical centers, whereas in routine clinical practice most parathyroidectomies are performed by surgeons with low annual case volumes ( 125 , 126 ), which may influence outcomes. Current guidelines recommend surgery in patients younger than 50 years based on a higher likelihood of disease progression compared with older individuals (65% vs. 23%). This age threshold, established in 1991, may warrant reconsideration in light of substantial gains in life expectancy, which is projected to increase by approximately 10 years by 2060 ( 127 ). Longer survival implies extended exposure to elevated PTH and calcium levels, and longitudinal studies have demonstrated bone mineral density deterioration in untreated patients up to 10 years after diagnosis ( 114 , 128 - 130 ). Collectively, these data suggest that expanding the age criterion to <60 years could modestly increase surgical eligibility while better reflecting contemporary demographic trends ( 94 ). In this context, delays in surgical referral among older adults, often driven by age, comorbidities, frailty, or mild disease presentation, may have important long-term implications. Although conservative management has not consistently been associated with excess mortality or morbidity in short- to mid-term studies, increasing life expectancy may alter the risk–benefit balance beyond previously studied time horizons.
