While patients with limited life expectancy or substantial operative risk may not be ideal candidates, the evidence supports the careful consideration of surgical intervention in appropriately selected individuals. Ultimately, management should adhere to established clinical guidelines and be individualized through shared decision-making that integrates patient preferences, anticipated longevity, and overall clinical context ( 28 ). TYPES OF SURGERY The conventional surgical approach (Kocher incision) involves the exploration of all four parathyroid glands under general or local anesthesia, with or without preoperative localization. This approach achieves surgical cure in more than 95% of cases. Another approach consists of removing a single gland without exploring all four glands. This is convenient because in 80 to 85% of PHPT cases, only one gland is involved. Another approach, which has become very common, is minimally invasive parathyroidectomy (MIP). It requires preoperative localization to remove only the abnormal gland. It is an easy approach for high-risk surgical patients and older adults. Intraoperative PTH measurement is used to verify the success of the surgery. If the PTH level drops by 50% at 10 minutes, the surgery is considered successful; otherwise, the procedure is converted to the conventional approach ( 96 ). Intraoperative PTH measurement in older adults is essential due to the high incidence of multiglandular disease ( 93 ). There is a small risk that the minimally invasive procedure may overlook other hyperactive glands that are suppressed in the presence of a dominant gland. The success rate is 95 to 98% ( 131 , 132 ). Minimally invasive parathyroidectomy results in a smaller incision, less cervical dissection and postoperative discomfort, shorter operative time, lower complication rates, shorter hospital stay, lower cost, and does not require general anesthesia ( 52 , 133 ). MIP can be performed with an endoscopic camera, but this provides no advantage other than a smaller incision ( 134 , 135 ). Non-Surgical Management of Primary Hyperparathyroidism Older patients who are not surgical candidates can be managed conservatively with close monitoring, especially when surgical risks outweigh the benefits. It is recommended to measure serum calcium, creatinine, and glomerular filtration rate annually. If renal stones are suspected, a urinary stone profile and renal imaging (X-ray, ultrasound, or CT scan) should be performed, along with an annual bone mineral density (BMD) scan.
