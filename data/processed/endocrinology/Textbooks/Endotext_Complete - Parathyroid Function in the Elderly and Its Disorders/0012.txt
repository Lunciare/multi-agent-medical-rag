Other contributing factors to urolithiasis include dehydration and elevated levels of calcitriol. Nephrocalcinosis is another renal manifestation, resulting from calcium phosphate deposition in the renal parenchyma. Some patients may also exhibit reduced creatinine clearance ( 67 ). Normocalcemic Primary Hyperparathyroidism Normocalcemic PHPT is defined as elevated PTH levels with normal total and ionized serum calcium, in the absence of secondary causes of PTH elevation such as vitamin D deficiency, chronic kidney disease with a glomerular filtration rate < 60 ml/min, use of medications such as thiazides, bisphosphonates, denosumab, and lithium, hypercalciuria, and malabsorption syndromes. The diagnosis should include measurements of total calcium, ionized calcium, and PTH on multiple occasions, with at least two additional measurements beyond the initial one over a period of 3 to 6 months. In 4–10% of cases, ionized calcium will be elevated, and up to 19% of patients will develop hypercalcemia within a 3-year follow-up period. Patients with normocalcemic PHPT should be monitored annually with calcium and PTH measurements, as well as bone mineral density assessments every 1 to 2 years. If hypercalcemia develops, current guidelines should be followed to determine whether surgical treatment is indicated or continued observation is appropriate. Conversely, if progression occurs at the skeletal level (e.g., fractures or progressive reduction in BMD) or at the renal level (e.g., nephrolithiasis or nephrocalcinosis), parathyroid surgery should be considered ( 68 ). Diagnosis The diagnosis of PHPT is biochemical and is confirmed by the presence of hypercalcemia in the setting of elevated or inappropriately normal parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels. Between 8% and 12% of patients with PHPT will have PTH values within the normal range, typically in the upper half; only about 1% will have PTH levels below 40 pg/mL ( 69 ). Additional laboratory and imaging findings include: Serum phosphorus is often at the lower limit of normal, with frank hypophosphatemia observed in approximately one-third of patients. Magnesium excretion is mildly increased, and some patients may exhibit mild hypomagnesemia. Vitamin D deficiency is common in patients with PHPT, particularly in mild cases (up to 50%), and may blunt hypercalcemia, resulting in high-normal serum calcium levels that can obscure or complicate the diagnosis.
