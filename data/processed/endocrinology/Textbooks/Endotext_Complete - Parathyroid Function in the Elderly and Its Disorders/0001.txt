Parathyroid Function in the Elderly and Its Disorders

ABSTRACT Aging is accompanied by profound alterations in calcium and bone metabolism, many of which arise from changes in parathyroid hormone (PTH) regulation, secretion, age-related modifications of the physiology of PTH, and target-tissue responsiveness. With advancing age, reductions in vitamin D synthesis, impaired renal activation of 1,25(OH) 2 D, and decreased intestinal calcium absorption contribute to a compensatory rise in PTH levels and a negative calcium balance. Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT), whose prevalence increases markedly after the sixth decade of life, may present as asymptomatic hypercalcemia, normocalcemic hyperparathyroidism, or classic symptomatic disease. Older adults frequently exhibit predominant skeletal involvement with a higher fracture risk, a complex interaction with metabolic syndrome, and a lower prevalence of nephrolithiasis, which is approximately 15% among patients older than 75 years. Surgical criteria for asymptomatic PHPT have been defined since 1991 and have undergone successive updates, most recently in 2022 at the Fifth International Workshop. Nevertheless, parathyroidectomy (PTX) continues to be underutilized in clinical practice, primarily driven by enduring misconceptions surrounding surgical risk. The chapter also addresses hypoparathyroidism, a less common but clinically challenging condition, highlighting the limitations of conventional therapy and the emerging role of PTH analogs. Understanding the interplay between aging physiology, comorbidities, and parathyroid dysfunction is essential for optimizing diagnosis, risk stratification, and treatment selection in elderly patients. PHYSIOLOGY OF PARATHYROID HORMONE Parathyroid hormone (PTH) is an 84-amino acid (aa) polypeptide hormone with a molecular weight of approximately 9,500 Daltons. It is synthesized as a 115-aa preprohormone in the chief cells of the parathyroid glands. In the endoplasmic reticulum, 25 aa are cleaved to produce a prohormone, and in the Golgi apparatus, an additional 6 aa are removed to generate the mature 84-aa PTH, which is then stored in secretory granules ( 1 ). PTH consists of an amino-terminal fragment comprising residues 1–34 (which confers biological activity) and a carboxy-terminal fragment comprising residues 50–84. The PTH gene is located on the short arm of chromosome 11 (11p15). Once stored in secretory granules, PTH can follow two fates: it is either secreted into the bloodstream in response to calcium concentrations or degraded by calcium-sensitive proteases into carboxy-terminal fragments (C-terminal fragments).
