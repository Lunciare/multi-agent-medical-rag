Genetics and Epigenetics of Central Precocious Puberty
KEYWORDS: puberty, cpp, precocious, precocious puberty, pubertal, pubmed, central, gene, central precocious, mutations, genetic, syndrome, girls, gnrh, genes


Normal onset of puberty typically occurs between ages 8 and 13 years in girls and 9 and 14 years in boys ( 5 ). This variation is influenced by genetic, nutritional, and environmental factors, reflecting the complex genetic architecture and regulatory pathways governing this developmental period ( 6 ). For example, racial differences may influence the timing of pubertal onset, with normal puberty beginning earlier in non-Hispanic black girls and, to a lesser extent, in Mexican-American girls ( 7 ) compared to Caucasian girls. Accordingly, some have proposed lower age thresholds to define precocious puberty in these specific populations. However, the validity of these race-specific recommendations has been questioned, and most pediatric endocrinologists continue to apply the traditional cutoffs ( 1 ). The onset of puberty is affected by several factors in addition to race; it occurs earlier in girls with a history of early maternal menarche, low birth weight, or excessive weight gain or obesity in infancy and early childhood, after international adoption (for unclear reasons, the risk is 10 to 20 times as great for these children ( 8 )), possibly after exposure to estrogenic endocrine-disrupting chemicals, or when no father is present in the household ( 9 ) . Other factors that may influence timing of puberty include sleep and nutritional status, which serve as critical modulators of pubertal timing and progression. Insufficient sleep duration and poor sleep quality have been consistently associated with an increased risk of early pubertal onset in both boys and girls ( 10 ), with evidence suggesting that late bedtimes and chronic sleep deprivation may accelerate pubertal development ( 11 ). This process may be mediated by increased adiposity and metabolic changes commonly associated with short sleep time. Nutritional status also exerts a profound influence on puberty. Both undernutrition and obesity can disrupt the normal tempo of pubertal development: chronic energy deficiency is linked to delayed or arrested puberty, while excess energy intake and higher body mass index are associated with earlier onset of puberty, especially in girls ( 6 , 12 ). The adipose-derived hormone, leptin, serves as a permissive signal for pubertal initiation, and other metabolic hormones such as insulin and ghrelin also modulate the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, further linking nutritional status to pubertal regulation ( 2 , 6 ).
