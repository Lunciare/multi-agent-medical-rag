In murine studies, loss of DLK1 expression results in increased NOTCH ½ (Notch Receptor ½) signaling, upregulation of HES1 (Hairy and Enhancer of Split 1) and HEY1 (Hairy/enhancer-of-split related with YRPW motif protein 1), and enhanced differentiation of kisspeptin neurons—leading to premature activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis ( 29 ). Loss-of-function mutations in DLK1 cause early puberty and have been associated with adverse metabolic outcomes, including obesity, type 2 diabetes, and dyslipidemia ( 14 ). DLK1 appears to regulate both the development of kisspeptin neurons and adipocyte differentiation. The clinical phenotype suggests that DLK1 may represent a novel link between reproduction and metabolism. Pathogenic loss-of-function variants in DLK1 were first described in families with familial CPP, where inheritance was consistently paternal, in line with the gene’s imprinting pattern ( figure 3 ). Clinical features in affected individuals included early pubertal onset (ages 4.5–6 years), advanced bone age, and elevated basal and stimulated LH levels. Girls with DLK1 mutations responded well to GnRH analog therapy and achieved near-normal adult height ( 32 ) . In a 2024 cohort study, sequencing of 174 children with CPP identified DLK1 mutations in approximately 4% of familial cases, confirming its role as the second most frequent monogenic cause of CPP after MKRN3 ( 14 ).
