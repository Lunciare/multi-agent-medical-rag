Hormonal improvement was limited: 15% improved, 70% remained stable, and 15% worsened ( 83 ). Lupi et al . performed a review of the literature and found somewhat better outcomes, reporting pituitary mass reduction in 84%, improved anterior pituitary function in 45%, and partial restoration of posterior function in 41% of cases treated with high-dose glucocorticoids and/or azathioprine. The relapse rate in this analysis was relatively low (14%) ( 94 ). A systematic review and individual patient data meta-analysis by Krishnappa et al. suggested that very high-dose intravenous glucocorticoids administered over longer periods may yield superior outcomes compared to oral or shorter-duration regimens ( 95 ). In a small prospective double-arm study, Chiloiro et al. reported better rates of hypophysitis resolution and pituitary function recovery in patients treated with high-dose glucocorticoids vs. observation alone. Predictors of favorable response included positive pituitary antibodies, presence of AVP deficiency or secondary hypogonadism at presentation, and specific MRI features (thickened stalk, smaller gland volume, absent posterior bright spot) ( 96 ). These findings warrant confirmation in larger prospective cohorts. More recently, Hacioglu et al. observed higher rates of hormonal and radiological improvement in patients receiving high-dose glucocorticoids compared to those managed conservatively. However, interpretation is limited by the study’s retrospective design and heterogeneous patient population ( 97 ). Whether AVP deficiency is an unfavorable prognostic factor for response to glucocorticoids is unclear. The abovementioned study by Chiloiro et al . suggests better outcomes in patients with AVP deficiency at the time of hypophysitis diagnosis ( 96 ); however, Lupi et al . found that patients with concomitant anterior and posterior pituitary dysfunction responded poorly to glucocorticoids, which were unable to reverse the hypopituitarism ( 98 ). Glucocorticoid therapy was also found to be less effective in granulomatous or xanthomatous hypophysitis ( 39 ). In glucocorticoid-resistant cases and when high-dose glucocorticoids cause unacceptable side effects, immunosuppressive drugs such as azathioprine, methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, and cyclosporin A have been used successfully in individual cases. However, the long-term effects are unclear ( 1 ). Rituximab has also been employed to treat steroid-refractory hypophysitis ( 40 , 99 - 101 ).
