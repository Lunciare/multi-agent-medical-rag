The AMH gene has been cloned in many other mammals ( 434 - 438 ), in the marsupial tammar wallaby ( 439 ), in the chick ( 440 , 441 ) and American alligator ( 442 ), all of which carry Müllerian ducts which regress in the male. The gene is also present in the caudate amphibian, Pleurodeles waltl, whose Müllerian ducts persist in males ( 443 ). Even more surprisingly, AMH orthologs ( 444 , 445 ) and the AMH type II receptor ( 446 ) have been cloned from the gonads of modern teleost fish, which do not possess Müllerian ducts at all. In fish, AMH appears to be involved essentially in germ cell proliferation and gonadal development (reviewed in ref. ( 447 )), which suggests that AMH was initially a regulator of gonadal differentiation which acquired its anti-Müllerian activity during the course of evolution without completely relinquishing its former role. Indeed, in higher vertebrates, AMH inhibits Leydig cell differentiation ( 448 ) and follicle maturation ( 449 ). The ontogeny of AMH expression differs widely between males and females. In the human fetal testis, AMH mRNA and protein can be detected from the 8 th week, when Sertoli cells begin to form cord-like structures, the future seminiferous tubules ( 208 ) ( Fig. 19 ). In the ovary, AMH production is detectable at 24 week gestation in granulosa cells of preantral follicles ( 289 ). The timing of the expression of AMH is crucial. In the male, high amounts of AMH must be expressed before Müllerian ducts lose their responsiveness, i.e. before the end of the 8 th week in the human fetus. In the female, to avoid destroying the reproductive tract, it must be expressed after the window of sensitivity of the Müllerian ducts to its action has closed. Thus, in both sexes, the initiation of AMH transcription is under tight transcriptional control.
