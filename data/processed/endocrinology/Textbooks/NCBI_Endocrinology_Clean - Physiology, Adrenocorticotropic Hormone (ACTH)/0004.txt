Classification is based on ACTH dependence. ACTH-dependent Cushing syndrome results from pituitary ACTH hypersecretion, termed "Cushing disease," or ectopic ACTH production by nonpituitary tumors, both of which drive cortisol overproduction. In contrast, ACTH-independent Cushing syndrome arises from exogenous glucocorticoid administration or autonomous cortisol secretion by an adrenal adenoma. The term "Cushing syndrome" encompasses all causes of cortisol excess, whereas Cushing disease specifically denotes pituitary ACTH overproduction. Cushing syndrome manifests with a broad spectrum of clinical features. Key signs that increase the likelihood of this diagnosis include facial plethora, violaceous striae, easy bruising, a rounded facial appearance (“moon face”), central adiposity, and proximal muscle weakness. Additional manifestations may include depression, impaired skin healing, insomnia, acne, osteopenia, decreased libido, weight gain, dorsocervical fat accumulation (“buffalo hump”), and hirsutism. Evaluation of Cushing syndrome requires a systematic approach. Clinical history should focus on identifying exogenous glucocorticoid use, which can contribute to hypercortisolism. In cases of exogenous steroid exposure, gradual dose reduction under close clinical monitoring is necessary to prevent adrenal insufficiency.
