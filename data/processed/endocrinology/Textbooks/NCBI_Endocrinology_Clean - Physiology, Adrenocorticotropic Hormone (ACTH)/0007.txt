When surgery is not feasible or the tumor recurs, alternative therapeutic strategies include repeat transsphenoidal surgery, radiotherapy for residual or recurrent pituitary tumors—often as adjuvant therapy—and medical treatment with steroidogenesis inhibitors such as metyrapone, ketoconazole, and osilodrostat. Novel pharmacologic agents, including the 11-β-hydroxylase inhibitor osilodrostat and the glucocorticoid receptor antagonist mifepristone, have been increasingly employed. Bilateral adrenalectomy is indicated in severe hypercortisolism requiring urgent control or disease refractory to other interventions. Postoperative management necessitates lifelong glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid replacement therapy. Ectopic Adrenocorticotropic Hormone Syndrome Ectopic ACTH syndrome arises from excessive ACTH production by nonpituitary tumors, most commonly neuroendocrine neoplasms of the thorax, with small-cell lung cancer representing the most frequent cause. Clinical presentation typically involves a rapidly developing hypercortisolism over several weeks, with manifestations similar to those observed in Cushing syndrome. Additional features often include hypokalemia and treatment-resistant hypertension. Evaluation of suspected ectopic ACTH syndrome focuses on identifying the causative tumor. CT imaging of the chest, abdomen, pelvis, and neck is generally recommended, with positron emission tomography considered when further localization is necessary.
