Physiology, Adrenocorticotropic Hormone (ACTH)

stimulates ACTH secretion from the anterior pituitary. ACTH then acts on the adrenal cortex to promote cortisol synthesis and release. ACTH secretion follows a diurnal rhythm, peaking in the early morning before awakening and declining gradually throughout the day. This pattern is disrupted in Cushing disease, with ACTH levels remaining elevated at night. The HPA axis is regulated by negative feedback, in which elevated cortisol suppresses ACTH and CRH secretion, thereby limiting further cortisol production. Conversely, low cortisol levels stimulate ACTH and CRH release, increasing cortisol synthesis. Pathophysiology Disorders associated with ACTH secretion may be classified by simultaneously evaluating cortisol and ACTH levels. Low cortisol with elevated ACTH suggests primary adrenal insufficiency, whereas low cortisol with low ACTH indicates secondary or tertiary adrenal insufficiency. When cortisol is elevated, measurement of ACTH distinguishes between ACTH-dependent and ACTH-independent causes. Elevated cortisol with elevated ACTH suggests ACTH hypersecretion due to a pituitary adenoma, as in Cushing disease, or an ectopic ACTH-producing tumor. Elevated cortisol with suppressed ACTH indicates autonomous cortisol production by an adrenal lesion, with feedback inhibition of pituitary ACTH secretion. Clinical Significance Cushing Syndrome Cushing syndrome is a rare disorder characterized by excessive cortisol production. Classification is based on ACTH dependence.
