Anatomy, Abdomen and Pelvis, Sphincter Urethrae
KEYWORDS: urethral, sphincter, urethral sphincter, incontinence, bladder, external, urinary, muscle, internal, urethra, external urethral, pelvic, internal urethral, urinary incontinence, detrusor

A malfunction of the urethral sphincters can lead to various disorders of the lower urinary tract, usually some form of incontinence. Urinary incontinence is at least twice as prevalent in women as in men. As women age, the risk of urinary incontinence tends to increase. Besides aging, this is due to constitutional issues, decreased mobility, genetic predisposing factors, vaginal childbirth history, pelvic floor muscle damage, pelvic organ prolapse, neuropathy, diabetes, hormonal changes, dementia, obesity, and menopause. There are five basic types of urinary incontinence. Stress incontinence is characterized by loss of urine during sneezing, coughing, or intense physical activity. Urge incontinence is associated with the sudden, uncontrollable need to urinate, which leads to an immediate loss of urine, commonly associated with detrusor overactivity. Overflow incontinence is frequent, intermittent dribbling of urine because the bladder is full and the patient is in urinary retention due to detrusor hypotonicity or bladder outlet obstruction. Functional incontinence is the inability to reach the restroom promptly due to mental or physical issues such as an obstruction or a long line to use the toilet.
