While obesity rates have increased considerably over the past 2 decades, this rapid increase in prevalence was not only associated with increased adiposity. Hypertension, dyslipidemia, and limited physical activity also increased insulin resistance. While there has been a rapid rise in pediatric obesity and type 2 diabetes, no consensus has been reached on the pediatric population's diagnostic criteria for insulin resistance. From a demographic standpoint, insulin resistance affects all races and ethnicities, with limited data on comparison between groups. Pathophysiology The 3 primary sites of insulin resistance are the skeletal muscle, liver, and adipose tissue. In a state of chronic caloric surplus, the tissues in the body become resistant to insulin signaling. Skeletal muscle is a large reservoir for circulating glucose, accounting for up to 70% of glucose disposal as measured by the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp. The direct result of muscle insulin resistance is decreased glucose uptake by muscle tissue. Glucose is shunted from muscle to the liver, where de novo lipogenesis (DNL) occurs.
