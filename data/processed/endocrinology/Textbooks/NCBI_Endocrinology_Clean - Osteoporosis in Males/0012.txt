In men, aging is associated with trabecular thinning (but not loss), whereas in women, aging is associated with perforations and trabecular bone loss. Contrary to women, where following menopause, a rapid reduction in estrogen levels occurs, a less abrupt change occurs in males, and most men can maintain normal androgen levels throughout their lives. Although infrequent, an accelerated process can occur in men, more commonly in those older than 70. Whether gonadal steroids play a role in age-related bone loss is less apparent. In certain situations with pursuant hypogonadism, eg, from an acute illness or commencement of medication (leading to a sudden reduction in testosterone levels), increased resorption of bone may occur, with rapid loss and risk of fractures similar to postmenopausal women not on estrogen therapy. That aging can lead to a reduction in sex hormone-binding globulin is well established, which can reduce free testosterone levels.
