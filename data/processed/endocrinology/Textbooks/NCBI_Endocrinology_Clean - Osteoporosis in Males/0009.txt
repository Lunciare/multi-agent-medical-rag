The increased androgens in males, as well as growth hormone and insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), additively stimulate the apposition of periosteal bone in men, whereas estrogens inhibit periosteal apposition in females, culminating in wider bones in men compared to women. The 2 most important considerations for bone density in an individual include peak bone mass and the rate of bone loss. Decreased bone mass may result from a reduction in pubertal bone growth or the acceleration of bone resorption following attainment of peak bone mass; both of these processes, to varying degrees, may likely be associated with osteoporosis in the individual male patient. The peak BMD is up to 10% higher in males than females; therefore, males have greater peak bone mass before the acceleration of bone resorption. Women demonstrate a younger onset of bone loss compared to men and undergo more rapid bone loss. Peak bone mass acquisition typically occurs around the third decade but can vary based on genetic, hormonal, and environmental factors.
