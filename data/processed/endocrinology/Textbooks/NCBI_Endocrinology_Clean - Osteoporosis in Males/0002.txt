Not until the 1820s, however, did a French pathologist, Jean Lobstein, coin the term osteoporosis as a distinctive medical entity. Although initially considered a disease of postmenopausal females, the burden of osteoporosis on males is of equal peril. Following a first fracture, men are more likely to experience a subsequent fracture compared to females and are at greater risk for morbidity and mortality. However, males experience a fracture roughly 1 decade later than females. Despite improved awareness of the condition in males, only around 10% of men with a diagnosis of osteoporosis appear to receive appropriate treatment; the disease continues to be underrecognized and mismanaged. Etiology Osteoporosis is classified into primary and secondary disease, with the latter accounting for up to 60% of cases in men. An estimated 20% of older osteoporotic men have hypogonadism. However, primary etiologies are often underestimated due to less active surveillance and screening in patients without typical risk factors. Primary osteoporosis can be further subcategorized into involutional, which refers to individuals older than 70 without an obvious risk factor, and idiopathic, which refers to patients younger than 70
