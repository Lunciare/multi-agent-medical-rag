Due to the loss of estrogen in Type 1, there is a shift towards dramatic bone loss (relative to bone formation) with the acceleration of trabecular bone loss (rather than cortical), involving regions with trabecular bone predominance, such as the vertebral body which has a trabecular-to-cortical ratio of 75:25, in comparison to the femoral neck at ratio of 30:70. Type 2 osteoporosis, also known as ‘senile’, occurs from low turnover and is noted to be a result of gradual trabecular and cortical bone loss (with mostly cortical predominance); it may be present in both sexes, typically in those over age 70. Subsequently, as osteoporosis was studied in further detail, it was apparent that it could also occur secondary to medication or underlying pathophysiological processes, for which the classification ‘secondary osteoporosis’ was created. Men are more likely to have a secondary cause for bone loss compared to women, estimated to be 50% to 80% compared to about 30%. Secondary osteoporosis risk factors are often classified as ‘modifiable’ or ‘non-modifiable’ (see Table. Risk Factors for Secondary Osteoporosis).
