Despite the absence of overt symptoms in many patients, up to 55% demonstrate subclinical nephrocalcinosis or nonobstructing renal stones. Among symptomatic patients, up to 75% present with acute renal colic or nephrolithiasis. Physical Examination Physical examination findings are frequently unremarkable, but should emphasize identifying complications related to hypercalcemia or alternative etiologies. Palpable parathyroid masses occur infrequently; however, a firm, fixed cervical mass in the setting of hypercalcemia warrants concern for parathyroid carcinoma. Associated manifestations of hyperparathyroidism and hypercalcemia include bone or joint pain, fractures, osteoporosis, muscle weakness, fatigue, abdominal pain, anorexia, constipation, depression, cognitive impairment, sleep disturbance, hypertension, cardiac arrhythmias, left ventricular hypertrophy, nephrolithiasis, nephrocalcinosis, and polyuria. Evaluation Patients presenting with clinical features or diagnostic findings of hypercalcemia should undergo evaluation for PHPT through laboratory testing (see Table 1 ). Diagnosis is confirmed when elevated serum calcium (total or ionized) coincides with an inappropriately high or normal PTH level measured simultaneously. Hypercalcemia is defined as total calcium greater than 10.2 mg/dL (2.55 mmol/L) or ionized calcium greater than 5.6 mg/dL (1.40 mmol/L).
