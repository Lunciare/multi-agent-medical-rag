Neuropsychiatric manifestations, including fatigue, depression, anxiety, cognitive slowing, and muscle weakness, are common and may improve following parathyroidectomy, although evidence for this improvement remains mixed. Chronic hypercalcemia can also cause gastrointestinal symptoms, eg, constipation, nausea, abdominal pain, dyspepsia, pancreatitis, or peptic ulcer disease, particularly in patients with MEN1-associated gastrinoma. Cardiovascular complications are more common in moderate to severe disease and may include hypertension, vascular calcification, left ventricular hypertrophy, and increased arterial stiffness, although the causal relationship is not well established. Surgical correction reduces calcium and PTH levels, but evidence regarding long-term reductions in cardiovascular risk remains inconsistent. Chronic hypercalcemia can also cause band keratopathy and soft-tissue calcifications. In the absence of surgical intervention, approximately one-quarter to one-third of patients experience disease progression over 10 to 15 years, developing worsening hypercalcemia, bone loss, or renal complications. In rare instances, hypercalcemic crisis may occur, characterized by severe hypercalcemia (>14 mg/dL), dehydration, altered mental status, and arrhythmias, necessitating urgent treatment. Complications Related to Surgery Although parathyroidectomy is curative in more than 95% of patients, it carries inherent surgical risks.
