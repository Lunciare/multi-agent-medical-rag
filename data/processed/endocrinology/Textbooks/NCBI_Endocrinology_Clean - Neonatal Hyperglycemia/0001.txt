Neonatal Hyperglycemia
KEYWORDS: hyperglycemia, glucose, insulin, neonatal, infusion, infants, preterm, levels, neonates, increased, sepsis, neonatal hyperglycemia, risk, diabetes, osmotic

Introduction Neonatal hyperglycemia is commonly defined as serum glucose greater than 150 mg/dL (8.3 mmol/L) or whole-blood glucose greater than 125 mg/dL (6.9 mmol/L), independent of gestational or postmenstrual age. A safe blood glucose target for neonates ranges from 70 to 150 mg/dL, with the upper limit set by the renal glucose threshold in preterm infants. Classification depends on timing after birth: early-onset hyperglycemia occurs within the first 48 hours of life, and late-onset hyperglycemia develops after the initial 48 hours. Etiology Prematurity and intrauterine growth restriction frequently contribute to neonatal hyperglycemia through inadequate insulin secretion and impaired suppression of hepatic glucose production. Increased insulin resistance further amplifies glucose elevation. Elevated levels of stress hormones, eg, epinephrine and norepinephrine, inhibit both insulin secretion and insulin action while simultaneously raising glucagon levels, which promote glycogenolysis. Catecholamine infusions, seizures, and physiologic stress from surgery, pain, hypoxia, respiratory distress, or sepsis commonly drive these hormonal changes. Feeding-related factors also influence glucose regulation. A delay in initiating enteral feeding reduces incretin secretion and, subsequently, promotes hyperglycemia, while the use of hyperosmolar formulas can trigger transient glucose intolerance.
