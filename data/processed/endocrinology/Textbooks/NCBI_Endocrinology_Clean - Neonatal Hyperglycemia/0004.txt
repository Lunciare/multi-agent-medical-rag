The most common cause is high exogenous glucose infusion rates in preterm infants who are already at risk for hyperglycemia due to the following reasons: decreased ability to suppress endogenous glucose production, decreased insulin response to glucose, and limited glycogen and fat stores. History and Physical No specific physical findings consistently point to hyperglycemia, although the examination may uncover indicators of an underlying condition, eg, temperature instability or low perfusion in cases of sepsis. Several nonspecific clinical features can suggest hyperglycemia, including increased urine output, dehydration, weight loss, fever, and feeding difficulty. These signs often reflect osmotic diuresis or the systemic effects of the contributing illness and warrant further evaluation to confirm elevated glucose levels and identify the precipitating cause. Evaluation The evaluation for neonatal hyperglycemia should include careful laboratory and clinical assessments. Relying solely on point-of-care blood glucose testing may produce inaccurate results, so elevated readings should be confirmed with serum glucose measurement before initiating treatment. Venous blood glucose provides more reliable values than capillary samples, as capillary glucose from heel-stick testing tends to be approximately 15% lower and is influenced by hematocrit levels.
