Delayed supplementation of parenteral amino acids slows the release of insulin-like growth factor-1, hindering beta-cell development and contributing to hyperglycemia. High intravenous lipid infusion rates increase free fatty acids, which competitively reduce glucose oxidation by supplying alternative substrates for oxidative metabolism; free fatty acids and glycerol further stimulate gluconeogenesis. Sepsis should be considered when hyperglycemia develops despite an unchanged glucose infusion rate, and necrotizing enterocolitis should also be assessed. Iatrogenic contributors, including errors in calculating the glucose infusion rate in intravenous fluids, frequently lead to excessive glucose delivery. Transient neonatal diabetes mellitus often affects small-for-gestational-age infants and follows a self-limited course. Several genetic syndromes also predispose infants to hyperglycemia, including Wolcott-Rallison syndrome, DEND syndrome (Developmental delay, Epilepsy, and Neonatal Diabetes), Rogers syndrome, and IPEX syndrome (Immune dysregulation, polyendocrinopathy, enteropathy, X-linked). Medication-related factors require equal attention. Maternal diazoxide exposure may cause hyperglycemia, hypotension, and tachycardia in neonates, while antenatal steroids may contribute to glucose elevation. Neonatal medications, eg, dopamine, dobutamine, epinephrine, caffeine, theophylline, phenytoin, and corticosteroids, frequently impair glucose regulation and promote hyperglycemia.
