Persistent dry eye with exposure keratopathy is observed in 1% to 8% of cases. Corneal ulceration, perforation, and endophthalmitis are present in very severe cases. Conjunctival chemosis is more common in moderate to severe cases and is seen in 15% to 25% of patients. Orbital Changes Hypertrophy of the orbital fat and extraocular muscles within the bony orbit raises the intraorbital pressure. Forward protrusion of the orbit causes proptosis. Constriction of the superior ophthalmic vein by enlarged extraocular muscles results in venous stasis. Venous congestion causes redness and swelling of the eyelids, chemosis, and caruncular edema. Dependent periorbital puffiness, often in the early morning, is also a sign of impaired venous outflow. Elevation of the episcleral venous pressure results in elevated intraocular pressure and open-angle glaucoma. Venous and lymphatic stasis in the orbit prolongs the half-life of inflammatory cytokines, causing a cycle of inflammation. Spontaneous fracture of the medial or inferior wall of the orbit resulting in spontaneous autodecompression.
