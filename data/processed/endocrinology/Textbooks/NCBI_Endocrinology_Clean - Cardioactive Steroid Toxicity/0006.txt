This accumulation of intracellular calcium triggers further calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, ultimately increasing myocardial contractility. In addition to increasing inotropy, CAS increases vagal tone in the sinoatrial and atrioventricular nodes, thereby decreasing conduction and shortening the refractory period, which increases the risk of automaticity and arrhythmias. Toxicokinetics Digoxin and digitoxin (not available in the United States) are the 2 primary CAS used clinically. They differ significantly in their pharmacokinetics. Digitoxin has higher oral bioavailability and greater protein binding (approximately 90%-95%). In contrast, digoxin is less protein-bound (20%-30%) but has a larger volume of distribution due to extensive tissue binding, particularly in skeletal muscle. Digitoxin undergoes predominantly hepatic metabolism and biliary excretion. In contrast, digoxin is eliminated primarily by renal excretion. As a result, renal impairment significantly increases the risk of digoxin toxicity, while digitoxin toxicity is less affected by kidney function. Both drugs have a narrow therapeutic index, requiring careful dose adjustment based on renal function (digoxin), age, drug interactions, and electrolyte status. Several medications increase digoxin levels by reducing renal clearance or altering distribution, including quinidine, amiodarone, verapamil, diltiazem, spironolactone, and carvedilol.
