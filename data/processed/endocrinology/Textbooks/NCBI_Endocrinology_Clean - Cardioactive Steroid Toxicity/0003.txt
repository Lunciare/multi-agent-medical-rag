Electrolyte disturbances, particularly hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, and hypercalcemia, enhance digoxin’s effects at the Na + , K + -ATPase pump and increase susceptibility to toxicity even at therapeutic serum concentrations. CAS toxicity may also occur following ingestion of plants or animal products containing cardiac glycosides. Common plant sources include foxglove, oleander, lily of the valley, and red squill. Certain herbal preparations and traditional medicines may contain cardioactive glycosides. Animal-derived toxins, such as bufadienolides found in Bufo bufo (common toad) and Rhinella margaritifera (Amozonian toad) toad secretions and preparations such as Ch'an Su, exert similar pharmacologic effects. Epidemiology CAS toxicity remains a clinically significant cause of cardiovascular poisoning worldwide. The American Heart Association notes that although prescribing of digoxin and digitoxin has declined in recent decades, toxicity continues to occur due to overdose, unintentional ingestion, drug–drug interactions, and accumulation in patients with impaired renal clearance. Older adults are disproportionately affected due to age-related declines in renal function and polypharmacy. Among patients aged 85 years and older, digoxin toxicity accounts for approximately 3.3% of emergency department visits and 5.9% of hospitalizations related to adverse drug events.
