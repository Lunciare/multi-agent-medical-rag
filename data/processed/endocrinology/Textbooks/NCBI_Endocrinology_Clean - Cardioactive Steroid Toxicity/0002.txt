Cardioactive Steroid Toxicity
KEYWORDS: digoxin, toxicity, cas, patients, renal, levels, clinicians, ingestion, digitoxin, risk, cas toxicity, fab, including, chronic, serum

CAS include digitoxin, ouabain, deslanoside, and lanatoside C. Historical accounts suggest that Vincent van Gogh’s use of digitalis-containing treatments may have contributed to xanthopsia (yellow visual discoloration), a factor proposed to have influenced the yellow tones in his paintings. Etiology CAS toxicity, most commonly due to digoxin, occurs in acute and chronic forms. Acute toxicity typically results from intentional overdose, accidental ingestion (particularly in children), dosing errors, or ingestion of plants containing cardioactive glycosides. Chronic toxicity more commonly develops in patients receiving therapeutic digoxin and is often due to impaired renal clearance, drug–drug interactions, electrolyte abnormalities, or changes in volume status that increase serum drug concentrations or tissue sensitivity. Digoxin is primarily eliminated unchanged by the kidneys, making renal insufficiency the most significant risk factor for chronic digoxin toxicity. Older age also increases risk due to reduced renal function, decreased lean body mass, and altered pharmacokinetics. Drug interactions, including those involving amiodarone, verapamil, quinidine, macrolides, and certain antifungal agents, can increase digoxin levels by reducing renal clearance or inhibiting P-glycoprotein–mediated transport. Electrolyte disturbances, particularly hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, and hypercalcemia, enhance digoxin’s effects at the Na + , K + -ATPase pump and increase susceptibility to toxicity even at therapeutic serum concentrations.
