Clinicians should be aware that chronic digoxin therapy produces characteristic ECG changes, including scooped ST-segment depression, T-wave flattening or inversion, shortened QT interval, and prominent U waves, that reflect therapeutic effects rather than toxicity. Given the CAS mechanism of action, virtually any arrhythmia may occur, although the combination of bradyarrhythmias or AV block with concurrent ventricular ectopy or tachyarrhythmias is highly suggestive of CAS toxicity. Bidirectional ventricular tachycardia, characterized by beat-to-beat alternation in QRS axis, is considered nearly pathognomonic. However, it may rarely occur in aconitine toxicity and in catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia. Younger individuals are more commonly affected by vagally mediated bradyarrhythmias. In contrast, patients with underlying structural heart disease are at greater risk of ventricular dysrhythmias. Treatment / Management Gastrointestinal Decontamination Initial management of CAS toxicity follows standard toxicologic principles, including stabilization of airway, breathing, and circulation. Because digoxin is rapidly absorbed, clinicians may consider gastrointestinal decontamination with activated charcoal if the patient presents early, particularly within 1 to 2 hours of ingestion. However, the benefit may extend beyond this window due to delayed gastrointestinal transit. Activated charcoal is administered orally at a dose of 1 g/kg.
