In situations where surgery is not feasible, other strategies, including medical management with somatostatin analogs, targeted therapies, or peptide receptor radionuclide therapy, are used for symptom relief and disease control. This discussion focuses specifically on GI-NETs originating in the midgut and hindgut, while foregut and thoracic NETs are considered separately. Etiology The etiology of GI-NETs is multifactorial and involves a combination of genetic, molecular, and environmental factors. Most GI-NETs are sporadic, and risk factors are not well understood. Approximately 20% of GI-NETs are inherited, such as multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1), von Hippel-Lindau disease (VHL), and neurofibromatosis (NF-1), and are associated with specific genetic mutations. Inherited GI-NETs are more commonly found in the foregut but are less likely to be responsible for midgut and hindgut GI-NETs. Risk factors associated with GI-NETs include a positive family history of cancer, while lifestyle and metabolic factors may contribute to the development of these tumors. Obesity and diabetes have been implicated as potential risk factors, possibly due to their association with chronic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation.
