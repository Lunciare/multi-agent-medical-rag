The Potential Role of Hypercortisolism in T2DM Cortisol, a glucocorticoid hormone, plays a central role in glucose metabolism by promoting hepatic gluconeogenesis, inhibiting peripheral glucose uptake, and inducing insulin resistance in muscle and adipose tissues ( 447 ). Prolonged exposure to high cortisol levels, as seen in conditions like Cushing’s syndrome or chronic stress, exacerbates these metabolic effects, leading to persistent hyperglycemia and compensatory hyperinsulinemia ( 448 ). Overtime, this may contribute to pancreatic beta- cell dysfunction and the progressive failure of insulin secretion. Additionally, cortisol promotes visceral fat accumulation, which further worsens insulin resistance through the release of pro-inflammatory adipokines and free fatty acids. Recently, in challenging to control T2DM patients, about 25% had hypercortisolism not diagnostic as Cushing’s, which may have contributed to hyperglycemia. Mifepristone, a glucocorticoid receptor antagonist, was shown to improve glycemic control in these challenging to treat T2DM ( 449 ). Additionally, mifepristone improves glycemic control and insulin sensitivity in patients with Cushing’s syndrome and may have therapeutic potential in T2DM. Summary: FFA and the Pathogenesis of Obesity and T2DM In obese individuals and in the majority (>80%) of subjects with T2DM, there is an expanded fat cell mass and the adipocytes are resistant to the anti-lipolytic effects of insulin ( 18 ). Most individuals with obesity or T2DM are characterized by visceral adiposity ( 272 ) and visceral fat cells have a high lipolytic rate, which is especially refractory to insulin ( 273 ). Not surprisingly, both T2DM and obesity are characterized by an elevation in the mean day-long plasma FFA concentration. Elevated plasma FFA levels, as well as increased triglyceride/fatty acyl CoA content in muscle, liver, and beta cell, lead to the development of muscle/hepatic insulin resistance and impaired insulin secretion.
