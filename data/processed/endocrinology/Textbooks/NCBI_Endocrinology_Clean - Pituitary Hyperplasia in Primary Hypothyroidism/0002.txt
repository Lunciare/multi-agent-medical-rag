Pituitary Hyperplasia in Primary Hypothyroidism

thyrotroph hyperplasia is what ultimately results in the enlargement of the pituitary gland. It is important to note that thyrotroph cells in the anterior pituitary comprise only 5% to 10% of all cell types in the gland, yet can cause significant gland enlargement in individuals with primary hypothyroidism. One postulated mechanism is that the number of thyrotroph cells can increase by up to 34% in patients with hypothyroidism. Epidemiology Certain studies have demonstrated that the incidence of pituitary hyperplasia in primary hypothyroidism can range anywhere from 25%-81%. These studies were based on skull x-ray findings showing increased sella turcica volume in those with primary hypothyroidism. Additional research has shown a correlation between pituitary hyperplasia, as measured by sella turcica size, and TSH levels. One study showed that in those patients with TSH levels above 50 IU/mL, 70% had enlargement of their pituitary gland. History and Physical History and physical examination can substantially guide further workup. The clinician needs to think systematically about end-organ deficiencies or excesses and their manifestations, as well as the local complications of an enlarged pituitary gland.
