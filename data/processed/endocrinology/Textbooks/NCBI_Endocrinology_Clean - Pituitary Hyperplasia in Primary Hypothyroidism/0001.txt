Pituitary Hyperplasia in Primary Hypothyroidism
KEYWORDS: pituitary, hypothyroidism, hyperplasia, primary, patients, tsh, primary hypothyroidism, pituitary hyperplasia, thyroid, elevated, levels, thyroxine, adenoma, hormone, gland

Introduction Pituitary hyperplasia is an under-recognized manifestation that can occur fairly commonly in primary hypothyroidism. It occurs due to the lack of negative feedback on the pituitary and hypothalamus. The presentation and imaging can mimic a pituitary mass. Patients can present with features of hypothyroidism, hyperprolactinemia, and even visual field defects if the hyperplastic pituitary gland compresses the optic chiasm. Recognition of this entity is important, as the primary modality of treatment is thyroid hormone replacement rather than surgery. Etiology In patients with primary hypothyroidism, the most common cause is chronic autoimmune thyroiditis or Hashimoto's thyroiditis, which results in a combination of cell and antibody-mediated thyroid destruction. As a result of thyroid destruction, low circulating thyroid hormone levels lead to reduced negative feedback on the hypothalamus, thereby elevating thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH). Elevated TRH levels lead to thyrotroph hyperplasia in the pituitary, which, in turn, results in increased thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) secretion. Elevated TRH levels can also lead to lactotroph hyperplasia, which accounts for the increased prolactin in these patients.
