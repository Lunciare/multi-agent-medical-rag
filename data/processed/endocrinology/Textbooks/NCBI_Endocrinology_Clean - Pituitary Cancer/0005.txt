Suspicion of pituitary carcinoma arises when patients experience neck or back pain, suggesting possible spinal metastases. Additionally, compression of nearby structures frequently contributes to clinical symptoms. The optic chiasm, cavernous sinus, and third ventricle represent common sites of involvement. Superior tumor growth through the diaphragma sella can compress the optic chiasm, producing bitemporal hemianopsia and diminished visual acuity. The cavernous sinus contains cranial nerves like III (oculomotor), IV (trochlear), V1 and V2 (trigeminal), and VI (abducens). Tumor compression of these nerves may cause ptosis, diplopia, or facial pain, while obstruction of venous drainage within the cavernous sinus can result in chemosis and ptosis. Involvement of the third ventricle may produce obstructive hydrocephalus. Large tumors (eg, macroadenomas) frequently cause nonspecific symptoms (eg, headaches) due to increased intrasellar pressure or dural stretching. Invasive pituitary tumors occasionally present with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage, seen as rhinorrhea. Hormone oversecretion-associated symptoms Various symptoms are related to the oversecretion of the following hormones: Prolactin: Excess prolactin may lead to impotence in men, while in women it can cause amenorrhea-galactorrhea syndrome. Growth hormone: Overproduction results in gigantism in prepubertal children and acromegaly in adults.
