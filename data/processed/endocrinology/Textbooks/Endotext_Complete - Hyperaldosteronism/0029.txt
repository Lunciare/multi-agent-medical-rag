TREATMENT OF SECONDARY ALDOSTERONISM Management of secondary aldosteronism focuses on treatment of the underlying cause and control of blood pressure, with the goal of preventing progressive renal dysfunction and cardiovascular morbidity. In fibromuscular dysplasia, percutaneous transluminal angioplasty without stenting is the treatment of choice and is frequently curative. In atherosclerotic renovascular disease, angioplasty with stent placement is generally preferred over angioplasty alone, particularly for ostial lesions ( 158 – 160 ). However, randomized controlled trials have not consistently demonstrated a survival or renal function advantage of revascularization over optimal medical therapy, underscoring the importance of careful patient selection. Aggressive medical therapy is appropriate for many patients and may be sufficient in a large proportion of cases. Given the central role of RAAS activation, ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers are first-line agents and provide both antihypertensive and renoprotective effects ( 158 – 160 ). Initiation of these agents requires close monitoring, as acute deterioration in renal function, particularly in the setting of bilateral renal artery stenosis, should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis and further evaluation. Surgical revascularization is now rarely indicated and is reserved for patients with failed endovascular interventions or complex anatomical considerations. All interventional procedures should be performed by experienced operators in high-volume centers. CONCLUSION Hyperaldosteronism represents a common and heterogeneous cause of hypertension with important cardiovascular and metabolic implications. Contemporary genetic, histopathological, and physiological insights have reframed PA as a disease spectrum ranging from overt unilateral or bilateral forms to milder and subclinical states. These advances underscore that inappropriate aldosterone activity, even in the absence of hypokalemia or severe hypertension, confers excess cardiovascular and renal disease risk. Assessment of ARR remains central to diagnosis, with increasing evidence supporting broader screening strategies to reduce underdiagnosis. Diagnostic pathways and confirmatory testing should be individualized and aligned with therapeutic intent. When appropriately applied, surgical and medical treatments are highly effective, with mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists constituting the cornerstone of therapy for bilateral disease and non-surgical candidates.
