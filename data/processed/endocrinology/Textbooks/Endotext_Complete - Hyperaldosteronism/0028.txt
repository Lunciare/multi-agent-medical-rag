Manifestations depend on the underlying etiology, severity of RAAS activation, volume status, and comorbid conditions. Consequently, diagnosis cannot rely on stereotypical clinical features and requires integration of biochemical, imaging, and clinical data. DIAGNOSIS OF SECONDARY ALDOSTERONISM AND RENOVASCULAR HYPERTENSION When clinical suspicion for renovascular hypertension exists and initial testing demonstrates normal or elevated plasma renin, further evaluation is warranted only if detection of a significant lesion would alter management. Clinical features that should raise suspicion include: Abrupt onset or worsening of hypertension. Unexplained or progressive renal dysfunction. Acute decline in renal function following ACE inhibitor or ARB initiation. Asymmetric kidney size. Young age with features suggestive of fibromuscular dysplasia. The diagnosis of renovascular hypertension requires both: Identification of a structural vascular lesion, and Evidence of functional renin excess from the affected kidney. Structural evaluation may be performed using CT angiography, MR angiography, or duplex Doppler ultrasonography. Renal arteriography remains the gold standard but is reserved for cases in which intervention is contemplated. No single non-invasive test definitively excludes renal artery stenosis, and test selection depends on clinical suspicion, renal function, local expertise, and resource availability. Functional significance of renal artery stenosis can be assessed using captopril renography, which exploits the dependence of glomerular filtration on angiotensin II–mediated efferent arteriolar constriction in ischemic kidneys. Although not recommended as a general screening test due to variable sensitivity, captopril renography can aid in determining whether a stenotic lesion is hemodynamically relevant and predictive of benefit from revascularization ( 127 , 156 , 157 ).
