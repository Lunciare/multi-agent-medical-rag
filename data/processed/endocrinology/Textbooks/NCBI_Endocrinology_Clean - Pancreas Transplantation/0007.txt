PAK is done in patients with insulin-treated diabetes who have a stable function after a previous renal allograft and meet the criteria for PTA. The number of pancreas transplants has decreased in the past decade, but the outcomes have improved for all groups of pancreas transplants, mainly due to improvements in immunosuppression, surgical technique, and donor-recipient selection. The decrease in pancreas transplants in the past decade can be due to improved insulin delivery systems, concerns about outcomes after solitary pancreas transplants, and a renewed interest in islet transplants. The most noticeable decrease is noted in the PAK and PTA groups, possibly due to decreased donors. Pancreas survival after PAK is less than the pancreas survival in SPK. As non-surgical diabetes management has improved, whether patients benefit from PTA has become unclear. The need for chronic immunosuppression and the risk of surgical complications make pancreas transplant an option only for a selected group of diabetic patients who have chronic renal failure, extrarenal complications, or struggle against difficult metabolic indices control.
