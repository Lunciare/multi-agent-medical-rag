Mild-to-moderate hypokalemia is typically treated with oral potassium supplements. Providing 60 to 80 mmol/day over days to weeks is usually sufficient. Oral supplementation can irritate the gastrointestinal mucosa and cause bleeding or ulceration. However, oral potassium replacement is associated with a lower risk of rebound hyperkalemia. Potassium chloride is the preferred formulation for replacement therapy in most cases. Increasing dietary potassium is usually inadequate to treat hypokalemia because most of the potassium in foods is coupled with phosphate. Most cases of hypokalemia involve chloride depletion and respond best to potassium chloride replacement. Intravenous (IV) repletion is administered if oral therapy is not tolerated. Replacement therapy must be provided rapidly when severe hypokalemia or clinical symptoms are present. Potassium chloride of 40 mmol given every 3 to 4 hours for 3 doses is preferred. Rapid correction is via oral intake, IV administration, or both. IV administration is preferred in the presence of cardiac dysrhythmias, digitalis toxicity, and recent or ongoing cardiac ischemia.
