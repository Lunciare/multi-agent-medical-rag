Doxazosin causes less tachycardia and postoperative hypotension, although no conclusive comparative studies exist. β-Adrenergic Blockade is administered only after adequate α-blockade has been achieved—never before, as this may trigger severe hypertensive crises. It is indicated if heart rate exceeds 80 bpm after starting α-blockade. The most used is propranolol, 30–40 mg/day divided into two or three doses. To restore circulating volume, 2–3 days after initiating α-blockade, sodium intake needs to be increased (5 g/day) if there is no congestive heart failure or renal insufficiency. Rapid volume replacement with 2,000 mL of normal saline over 12 h prior to surgery is frequently used. Calcium channel blockers are not recommended as monotherapy but can be used when there is intolerance to α-blockers, paroxysmal hypertension, ischemic heart disease, normotension, or mild preoperative hypertension. Most used drugs are nifedipine (30–60 mg/day), verapamil (120–240 mg/day), and nicardipine (20–40 mg/day). The Tyrosine Hydroxylase Inhibitors (Metyrosine) are rarely used. If using metyrosine, start 250 mg every 6 h, increasing to 1 g every 6 h the day before surgery. Pheochromocytoma: Specific Intra- and Postoperative Complications During surgery for pheochromocytoma, several intraoperative complications may arise. Hypertensive crises should be managed with intravenous nitroprusside or phentolamine, while supraventricular arrhythmias are treated with intravenous propranolol and ventricular arrhythmias with intravenous lidocaine. The use of halothane, which can induce dysrhythmias, as well as succinylcholine and pancuronium, which can release histamine, or other catecholamine-releasing agents, is not recommended. Post-resection hypotension is another potential complication and should be treated with aggressive volume replacement, though caution must be exercised due to the risk of heart failure. In certain cases, infusion of adrenergic drugs may be indicated. Postoperatively, hypotension may occur because of hemorrhage, inadequate volume replacement, heart failure, or insufficient steroid replacement, particularly if the adrenalectomy is bilateral. Conversely, hypertension may result from hypervolemia due to excessive perioperative volume administration used to counteract post-resection hypotension, from an unrecognized pheochromocytoma—though this is very rare today—or from inadvertent ligation of the renal artery. Additionally, hypoglycemia is observed in approximately 10% of cases ( 98 - 111 ). ADRENALECTOMY Laparoscopy-based minimally invasive surgery has transformed adrenal surgery.
