Evaluation of adrenal incidentalomas requires assessment of both structure and function. Autonomous hormone secretion is found in approximately 10–15% of cases, and 10–15% of incidentalomas are bilateral. After excluding metastases from non-adrenal primary tumors, the majority of adrenal incidentalomas are benign adrenocortical adenomas (approximately 80%), including cortisol and aldosterone producing adenomas. Less common etiologies include pheochromocytomas (<5%) and adrenal carcinomas (<2%) ( 35 – 37 ). Malignancy risk assessment is essential. The likelihood of ACC and metastases (MTS) is generally <2% and 0.7–2.5%, respectively, but increases with tumor size >4 cm, high CT density (>20 Hounsfield units), older age, male sex, and non-incidental detection ( 39 ). Positron emission tomography (PET-CT) using 18-fluoro-deoxyglucose (18F-FDG) is useful in selected cases, particularly when MTS is suspected, as malignant lesions typically show increased radiotracer uptake. Functional hormonal evaluation in older adults follows the same principles as in younger individuals. While approximately 90% of adrenal incidentalomas are non-functioning, up to 20% may be associated with mild autonomous cortisol secretion (MACS), previously referred to as subclinical Cushing’s syndrome. When evaluating an adrenal incidentaloma, initial screening tests include the overnight 1 mg dexamethasone suppression test (with dexamethasone taken at 11 p.m. the night before), the plasma aldosterone concentration/plasma renin activity (PAC/PRA) ratio or direct renin concentration, and plasma metanephrines (or less preferably, 24-hour urinary metanephrines). If any of these tests are abnormal, further dynamic studies for pheochromocytoma, primary hyperaldosteronism, or Cushing syndrome may be required ( 35 – 37 ). Adrenalectomy is indicated if hormonal hypersecretion is confirmed, if radiological features suggest malignancy or pheochromocytoma, or if the mass is larger than 4 cm in diameter. Adrenalectomy may be warranted if imaging with 18F-FDG PET-CT or I-123 MIBG SPECT raises suspicion for malignancy. In cases of MACS, surgical treatment is controversial but may offer benefits depending on the presence of comorbidities. If the mass is non-functioning, a repeat non-contrast CT scan is recommended after 6–12 months. If the diameter remains less than 4 cm, continued follow-up with annual hormonal evaluation is advised, although the optimal frequency of follow-up is not well established.
