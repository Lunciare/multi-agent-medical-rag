Results may be influenced by coexisting illnesses—particularly common in older adults—which can impair the HPA axis. The most widely accepted diagnostic approach involves measuring serum cortisol after the 1-mg DST. Interpretation of results is as follows: 5 μg/dL: Diagnostic for endogenous hypercortisolism or CS. 1.8–5 μg/dL: Suggestive of MACS. Baseline ACTH is typically suppressed or in low-normal range in MACS. If clinical suspicion of CS remains, confirmatory testing is recommended and may include 24-hour urinary free cortisol, late-night salivary or serum cortisol, or morning cortisol following administration of 8 mg of oral dexamethasone the night before. < 1.8 μg/dL: Endogenous hypercortisolism can be ruled out. Some authors also recommend evaluating baseline serum DHEAS, as low levels may support a diagnosis of ACTH-independent hypercortisolism due to chronic suppression. However, its diagnostic value in older adults may be limited due to the natural age-related decline in DHEAS ( 30 ). Management of MACS remains controversial. Nevertheless, current evidence suggests that untreated MACS may contribute to an increased risk of cardiovascular events, overall mortality, and vertebral fractures. Treatment decisions should be individualized based on patient age, comorbidities, and disease progression. In patients younger than 50 years, adrenalectomy should be considered. In individuals aged 70 years or older, clinical monitoring is generally recommended, particularly when radiological findings suggest benign features. For patients between 50 and 70 years of age, the presence of comorbid conditions should guide the therapeutic approach. If MACS is stable and well-controlled in these patients, ongoing monitoring may be appropriate. This approach may also be suitable in cases with multiple comorbidities and a history of cardiovascular events, as a prolonged disease course and established organ damage are predictors of limited improvement following surgery. However, a growing body of evidence supports the benefit of adrenalectomy in improving obesity and metabolic parameters, hypertension, and diabetes control, as well as reducing vertebral fracture risk and possibly improving cognition ( 49 – 54 ). In addition, if the condition is of recent onset, shows evidence of progression, or proves difficult to manage, adrenalectomy may be the preferred course of action. Even when hypercortisolism is mild, its persistence can lead to changes associated with premature aging (frailty) and increased mortality.
