Non-human primate models suggest the zona reticularis may be more susceptible to aging-related changes, and this decrease may be mediated by downregulation of adrenal lipid metabolism ( 4 , 9 ). The role of altered adrenal lipid metabolism in adrenal aging is further supported by findings of lipid depletion and telomere shortening in zona reticularis cells of patients who died after prolonged critical illness ( 10 ). While these steroids are markers of biological aging, their decline does not appear to significantly affect clinical outcomes. Supplementation with DHEA has not demonstrated improvements in insulin sensitivity, muscle strength, or oxygen consumption in controlled trials ( 11 ). Changes in Adrenomedullary Function Aging is associated with increased plasma and urinary norepinephrine levels, primarily due to heightened nocturnal secretion. This results from sympathetic nervous system activation and diminished peripheral receptor sensitivity. Although epinephrine levels may remain stable or decline slightly, the net effect includes a substantially attenuated epinephrine response to acute stress and reduced metabolic clearance of catecholamines ( 4 , 11 ). PRIMARY ADRENAL INSUFFICIENCY IN THE ELDERLY Autoimmune adrenalitis is uncommon in adults under 50 and becomes less frequent with age. However, the overall prevalence of primary adrenal insufficiency is higher in individuals over age 60 ( 12 ). In older adults, causes such as adrenal tuberculosis, infiltrative diseases (e.g., amyloidosis), bilateral adrenal hemorrhage, or necrotizing adrenalitis due to cytomegalovirus (CMV) are more common. Bilateral adrenal hemorrhage may arise in patients undergoing anticoagulation or experiencing coagulopathy and sepsis ( 13 – 17 ). The presentation of primary adrenal insufficiency (PAI) in elderly patients is often insidious. Symptoms include decreased functional capacity, weight loss, gastrointestinal disturbances, and hyponatremia—primarily due to cortisol deficiency and inappropriate antidiuretic hormone (ADH) secretion. Classic signs like hyperpigmentation are less common. The diagnosis may be further complicated by the co-occurrence of oligosymptomatic infections, which are common in this age group. Notably, PAI increases cardiovascular and infection risk and contributes to osteoporosis ( 15 , 18 , 19 ). The diagnosis of PAI should be suspected when serum morning cortisol is less than 5 mcg/dL.
