Physiology and Diseases of the Adrenal Glands in Older Adults
KEYWORDS: adrenal, pubmed, patients, primary, adrenalectomy, surgery, treatment, clinical, age, cortisol, elderly, endocrinol, free, hypertension, article


ABSTRACT The adrenal glands are crucial to the maintenance of physiological homeostasis, contributing to the regulation of stress responses, electrolyte balance, and metabolic processes. This chapter provides an in-depth examination of the anatomical and physiological modifications in adrenal function that occur with aging and the implications of these changes for clinical assessment. It further reviews the clinical characteristics, diagnostic evaluation, and therapeutic management of major adrenal disorders in older adults, with particular attention to the blunted clinical manifestations and the need for individualized approaches in the presence of comorbid conditions in elderly patients. The chapter also outlines advances in adrenal imaging and summarizes recent guideline updates relevant to the evaluation and treatment of adrenal diseases. Finally, it highlights the importance of personalized management strategies that consider underlying medical conditions, functional status, and overall quality of life. I NTRODUCTION The adrenal glands play a vital role in the regulation of numerous physiological processes, including stress response, electrolyte balance, and metabolism. In the elderly population, changes in adrenal gland function become increasingly relevant as they contribute to a spectrum of clinical manifestations and chronic health conditions. This chapter reviews the anatomical and physiological alterations in adrenal function associated with aging, as well as the clinical characteristics, diagnosis, and treatment of major adrenal disorders in older adults ( 1 ). AGE-RELATED CHANGES IN ADRENAL FUNCTION With advancing age, several significant modifications occur in the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. Cortisol secretion increases by 20–50% over a 24-hour period, and the diurnal rhythm of cortisol shows an earlier nadir ( 2 ). These alterations may impair feedback inhibition, resulting in a prolonged response to stress, and have been associated with increased visceral adiposity, bone mineral density loss in men, and memory decline in women and possibly men ( 3 – 5 ). Aldosterone secretion also changes markedly with aging, largely due to a primary decline in renin activity, which can lead to an approximately 50% reduction in aldosterone production by age 70. Consequently, elderly patients may experience increased natriuresis, hyponatremia, and hyperkalemia, particularly in the context of renal insufficiency ( 6 ). Dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) and its sulfate form (DHEAS), precursors to sex steroids, decrease progressively beginning in the third decade of life.
