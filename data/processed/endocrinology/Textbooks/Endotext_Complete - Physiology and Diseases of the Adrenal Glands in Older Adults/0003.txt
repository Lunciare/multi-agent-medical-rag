Baseline ACTH, plasma renin activity (PRA), and aldosterone levels provide early diagnostic clues. A twofold elevation in ACTH with elevated PRA and low aldosterone confirms primary adrenal insufficiency ( 13 , 14 ). Laboratory evaluation must consider factors affecting cortisol-binding globulin (CBG), such as inflammatory states, liver disease, nephrotic syndrome, and malnutrition. In elderly women, CBG levels may be elevated due to estrogen use ( 13 , 15 , 18 ). Interestingly, DHEAS can be especially valuable in patients with indeterminate baseline cortisol (5-9.9 mcg/dL). If DHEAS is ≥60 mcg/dL, AI is unlikely, so dynamic testing such as cosyntropin stimulation test might not be needed in those patients. However, as DHEAS production declines with age, it is not recommended to use this as diagnostic marker for AI in elderly patients ( 20 ). To determine the etiology, testing for anti-21-hydroxylase antibodies is recommended. In addition, clinicians should evaluate for conditions that predispose to adrenal hemorrhage, such as anticoagulation or antiphospholipid syndrome. CT imaging can reveal adrenal atrophy (suggestive of autoimmune disease), calcifications (suggestive of tuberculosis), or mass lesions. In complex cases, PET-CT with FDG may help differentiate metastasis from primary adrenal disease. PAI may be associated with autoimmune polyglandular syndromes (APS), though this is rare in the elderly. Conditions like adrenoleukodystrophy, familial glucocorticoid resistance, and congenital adrenal hyperplasia are also typically diagnosed earlier in life ( 13 , 15 , 17 , 18 ). When treating PAI in older adults, several important factors must be considered in hormone replacement therapy. These include age-related changes in drug metabolism, potential drug interactions due to polypharmacy, a higher prevalence of comorbid conditions, and an increased risk of acute adrenal insufficiency—often triggered by infections that may present with fewer symptoms. Cognitive impairment may also affect medication adherence and the ability to understand disease management, including appropriate use of stress dosing. Given the excellent therapeutic response to steroid hormone replacement, treatment should begin promptly upon clinical suspicion. Glucocorticoid replacement therapy for adrenal insufficiency in older adults requires careful individualization to achieve symptom control while minimizing adverse effects. Oral hydrocortisone remains the standard first-line treatment, typically given at a total daily dose of 15–25 mg divided into two or three doses.
