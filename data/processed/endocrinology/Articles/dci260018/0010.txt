Additionally, AID systems appear to be effective in people with type 2 diabetes on insulin therapy. In some countries, such as the U.S., modified versions of CGM are available for over-the-counter (OTC) use. These de­ vices do not have the alarms or alerts of the prescription-requiring devices and may be preset for different glucose ranges; however, they are effective for people who wish to pay out-of-pocket and monitor their glucose levels. Increas­ ing data exist to guide individuals and clinicians as to whether CGM data reflect normal or abnormal glucose tolerance (28,29). Additionally, studies are begin­ ning to show benefits from the use of CGM in lifestyle programs, often in con­ junction with other wearable technolo­ gies (30–32). Recent meta-analyses have shown that, compared with self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG), the use of CGM is associ­ ated with better glucose management in people with type 2 diabetes, both insulin and noninsulin treated (33–36).
