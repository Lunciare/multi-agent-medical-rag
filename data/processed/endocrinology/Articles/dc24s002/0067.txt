The recent ADA-European Association for the Study of Diabetes type 1 diabetes con- sensus report recommends that regardless of current age, individuals diagnosed un- der 6 months of age should have genetic testing (36). Correct diagnosis has critical implications, because 30–50% of people with KATP-related neonatal diabetes will ex- hibit improved blood glucose levels when treated with high-dose oral sulfonylureas instead of insulin. Insulin gene (INS) mu- tations are the second most common cause of permanent neonatal diabetes, and while intensive insulin management is currently the preferred treatment strategy, there are important genetic counseling considerations, as most of the mutations that cause diabetes are dominantly inherited. Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young MODY is frequently characterized by onset of hyperglycemia at an early age (classically before age 25 years, although diagnosis may occur at older ages). MODY is charac- terized by impaired insulin secretion with minimal or no defects in insulin action (in the absence of coexistent obesity). It is in- herited in an autosomal dominant pattern with abnormalities in at least 13 genes on different chromosomes identiﬁed to date (162).
