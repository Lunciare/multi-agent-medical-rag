As with the glucose measures, several prospective studies that used A1C to pre- dict the progression to diabetes as deﬁned by A1C criteria demonstrated a strong, continuous association between A1C and subsequent diabetes. In a systematic re- view of 44,203 individuals from 16 cohort studies with a follow-up interval averaging 5.6 years (range 2.8–12 years), those with A1C between 5.5% and 6.0% (between 37 and 42 mmol/mol) had a substantially in- creased risk of diabetes (5-year incidence from 9% to 25%).Those with an A1C range of 6.0–6.5% (42–48 mmol/mol) had a 5-year risk of developing diabetes be- tween 25% and 50% and a relative risk 20 times higher than that with A1C of 5.0% (31 mmol/mol) (81). In a commu- nity-based study of African American and non-Hispanic White adults without diabetes, baseline A1C was a stronger predictor of subsequent diabetes and cardiovascular events than fasting glu- cose (82).
