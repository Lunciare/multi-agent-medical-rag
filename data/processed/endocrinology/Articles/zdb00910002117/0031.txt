It is important to note however, that the reduction in muscle mitochondrial ca- pacity does not (yet) affect the capability of the body to enhance fat oxidation. This is an important ﬁnding since it has generally been assumed that a reduction in muscle mitochondrial function will result in reductions in whole- body fat oxidative capacity (3–5,42,43). Here we show that this extrapolation may not be justiﬁed, although we cannot exclude that the fasting-induced reduced mitochondrial function in muscle may decrease muscle-speciﬁc fat oxi- dation compensated by increased fat oxidation in other organs, or may have an impact on the capacity to switch from carbohydrate to fat oxidation (metabolic ﬂexibility). In conclusion, 60 h of fasting in humans lowered insulin- stimulated glucose uptake down to 50% along with drasti- cally elevated plasma FFA and IMTG levels. This was accompanied by an overall reduction in intrinsic mitochon- drial functional capacity in skeletal muscle, despite a pro- nounced increase in whole-body fat oxidation. Since prolonged fasting is a physiologic condition in which in- creased fat oxidation becomes very important, a reduced mitochondrial function seems unbeneﬁcial from a physio- logic point of view.
