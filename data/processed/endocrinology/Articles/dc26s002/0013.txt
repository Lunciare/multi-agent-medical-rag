For example, if an individual meets the di­ agnostic criterion for A1C (two results ≥6.5% [≥48 mmol/mol]) but not FPG (<126 mg/dL [<7.0 mmol/L]), that per­ son should nevertheless be considered to have diabetes. Test results close to the diagnostic threshold should prompt the health care professional to educate the individual about the onset of possible symptoms of hyperglycemia and to repeat the test in 3–6 months. Consistent and substantial discordance between glucose values and A1C test re­ sults should elicit additional follow-up to determine the underlying reason for the discrepancy (including evaluation for the possibility of an analytical problem or in­ terference with either test) and its clini­ cal implications, if any, for the individual (Table 2.3). In this context, alternative validated biomarkers of chronic hypergly­ cemia such as fructosamine and glycated albumin should be considered for moni­ toring glycemic management in people with diabetes.
