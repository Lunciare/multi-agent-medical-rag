A1C reflects glucose bound to hemo­ globin over the life span of the erythro­ cyte (∼120 days) and is thus a “weighted” average that is more heavily affected by recent blood glucose exposure. Thus, clini­ cally meaningful changes in A1C can be seen in <120 days. A1C is an indirect measure of glucose exposure, and fac­ tors that affect hemoglobin concentra­ tions or erythrocyte turnover can affect A1C (e.g., thalassemia or folate deficiency) (Table 2.3). A1C may not be a suitable di­ agnostic test in people with anemia, peo­ ple treated with erythropoietin, or people undergoing hemodialysis or HIV treatment (1,17). Some A1C assays can be affected by hemoglobin variants. For instance, an A1C assay without interference from hemoglobin variants should be used in in­ dividuals with sickle cell trait. An updated list of A1C assays with interferences is avail­ able at ngsp.org/interf.asp.
