MEN1 can be diagnosed in 3 ways: (1) autosomal dominant genotype-positive MEN1, deﬁned by identiﬁcation of a pathogenic or likely pathogenic germline MEN1 gene (PV/LPV) variant leading by the manifestation of 2 cardinal MEN1 features without genetic conﬁrmation; or (3) familial MEN1, deﬁned by presence of an MEN1-associated tumor in a patient who has a ﬁrst-degree relative with MEN1. A diagnosis of MEN1 has important implications for family members because ﬁrst-degree relatives have a 50% risk of inheriting the condition. While patients with MEN1 have tended to live longer in recent years, they still have shortened overall life expectancies compared with the general population. Two-thirds of patients with MEN1 syndrome die from an MEN1-related cause, and advanced pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (pNETs) are the most common disease-related cause of death in 40% to 45% of cases.4 Timely identiﬁcation and intervention of MEN1-associated neoplasms are needed to reduce the morbidity and mortality associated with this disease. However, the frequency and nature of interventions must also consider the direct medical costs, iatrogenic harms, and psy- chological burdens to affected patients and families.
